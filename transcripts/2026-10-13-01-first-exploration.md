**ASSISTANT — 02**

Yes—and I think **“a sense of place” is a much stronger starting point than “proving AI can make original art.”** It gives you something to investigate, rather than an argument the work has to win.

The distinction you’re making is important:

**Not a picture of Bradford-on-Avon generated by AI. A portrait of Bradford-on-Avon as it exists within a model.**

Those are very different projects. The second could be beautiful, strange and revealing without resembling the town at all.

## What would it mean to see through an LLM’s eyes?

You can get partway towards this, but there isn’t a hidden photograph to uncover. For a text model, the material you can work with is numerical: patterns of internal activity, relationships between representations, and the way those patterns change in different contexts. Concepts are distributed across the network rather than stored in neatly labelled compartments. ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/mapping-mind-language-model))

**The interesting thing is that this isn’t entirely metaphorical.** Researchers studying Llama 2 recovered geographic structure by fitting simple mathematical probes to its internal activations. They weren’t asking it to draw a map; they were measuring information represented inside the model. That demonstrates a real basis for investigating learned representations of place—although it doesn’t establish what any particular model represents about Bradford-on-Avon. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02207))

Anthropic also identified an internal feature associated with the Golden Gate Bridge, and examined related features nearby in its representational space. That is much closer to your idea than asking a chatbot, “What do you imagine when you think of this place?” ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/mapping-mind-language-model))

That last distinction matters: **a model describing its own imagination is still producing a description, not giving you direct access to its internals.**

I’d think of your role as inventing an instrument that translates some of those internal measurements into something perceptible. Like choosing how to render a scientific scan: the measurement is real, but the colours, composition, material and emphasis are authored.

You wouldn’t be claiming to reveal its literal vision. You’d be making an artistic interpretation of something you actually measured.

## Why Bradford-on-Avon could be a compelling subject

Because you have a lived relationship with it.

Your sense of the town might include a particular walk, an awkward junction, a change in light, somewhere you used to go, something insignificant that has become important through repetition.

That gives you a question:

> **What is the difference between knowing about a place and knowing it?**

Imagine an abstract landscape in which some associations become enormous, while things that matter deeply to you barely register. Or one where physically distant places sit beside each other because their representations are similar.

Those are possibilities to investigate, not findings about Bradford-on-Avon. But the mismatch could become the subject of the work—not a defect you need to correct.

I’d be interested in a series with a working premise like **“Places Never Visited.”** Not artificial travel photography, but studies of places represented without having been experienced.

### Three directions I would explore

**1. A landscape of associations**

Investigate how the town’s internal representation relates to other places and concepts. Then use those measured relationships to construct a composition.

Proximity could determine where forms gather. Similarity could determine which lines connect. Differences across carefully controlled prompts could introduce movement or instability.

The important part is that you don’t decide beforehand that “river” means blue waves and “history” means brown squares. You let the measurements impose structure, then develop your own visual language around them.

It could feel topographic without being a geographic map: an associative terrain rather than a street plan.

**2. A place that won’t hold still**

Present the same place name in several neutral, equivalent contexts and measure which internal patterns persist and which change.

Stable patterns could produce continuous lines; variable ones could split, interrupt or disperse. A series of prints could show a place repeatedly assembling itself, never quite identically.

I’d call this a study of *stability*, rather than “what the AI knows.” Consistency isn’t proof of truth, and an absent signal in your chosen measurement wouldn’t prove the model knows nothing. But persistence and instability are already interesting artistic materials.

**3. A place encountering someone who lives there**

Start with the model’s response to the place name alone. Then introduce your own firsthand observations, one at a time, and measure how the representation shifts.

Not a tourist description. Something specific and ordinary that you have actually noticed.

The resulting work could show an abstract place being disturbed by lived experience. That second stage would be an intentional intervention—adding context, not revealing something that was already there, and not necessarily retraining the model.

This is probably the direction I find most emotionally interesting: **the difference between an inherited representation and an encounter.**

## How you could genuinely get the material

There are two different routes hiding inside “the actual trained data.”

**The training material itself** means the documents used to train the model. **The learned representation** means the patterns the model developed through that training. They are related, but they aren’t interchangeable.

For a project built around actual source documents, you would choose a model whose training data is available. Ai2’s Olmo 3, for example, was released with an open training-data curriculum. That gives you a route to investigating source material, rather than trying to infer which documents a closed model encountered. ([Allen AI](https://allenai.org/blog/olmo3?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

But **the learned representation sounds closer to what you’re reaching for.**

A practical starting point would be an inspectable, open-weight model with existing interpretability tools. Google’s **Gemma Scope** provides tools for examining internal representations in Gemma models, including methods that decompose activity into more interpretable features. ([Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/models/gemma/gemma-scope/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) **Neuronpedia** lets you run your own text through supported models and inspect which features activate, including filtering by token and layer. That is actual model inference—not an image search and not a chatbot inventing an account of its thoughts. ([docs.neuronpedia.org](https://docs.neuronpedia.org/search))

For an initial experiment, I would keep the setup deliberately spare:

- **One fixed model, no browsing or supplied descriptions.** Start with the place name in a few controlled templates.
- **A comparison set.** Include other real places and invented place names, to test whether you are measuring something distinctive rather than a generic reaction to town-like wording.
- **One visual system.** Translate the recorded measurements into a small family of drawings using the same rules.

The first question is whether there is an interesting, distinguishable signal—not whether we can immediately produce a beautiful print. We haven’t established that signal for Bradford-on-Avon yet.

After that, I’d be tempted by restrained physical work: fine lines, layered marks, perhaps plotted drawings. Not because it has to look technical, but because it would let you explore this without defaulting to the visual language of glowing AI clouds.

## Where the originality would sit

For me, your authorship would be in **what you investigate, how you measure it, how you translate it, and what you choose to make visible**.

The model doesn’t need to be an autonomous artist for the work to be original. It can be the material and the subject.

But I also wouldn’t make the series responsible for declaring AI ethically uncomplicated. Making an original artwork and resolving the provenance of a model’s training material are separate questions. The work could hold that tension rather than trying to defeat it.

My main artistic test would be:

> **Does the model meaningfully determine the form, or is it just providing numbers to decorate an otherwise arbitrary pattern?**

There is always interpretation in the mapping. The aim isn’t to remove you from the process. It’s to make the relationship between the measured behaviour and the resulting artwork consequential.

**The strongest version, to me, is an attempt to encounter something familiar through a genuinely unfamiliar representation—and then reveal what that representation preserves, distorts or leaves unresolved.** That feels worth exploring on its own terms, not merely as a rebuttal to AI slop.

## The important distinction: AI as material, not art director

I wouldn’t start by asking an LLM what Bradford-on-Avon should look like, what colours represent it, or what kind of pattern to generate.

> **What structures can I extract from a model’s representation of this place, and what happens when I give those structures a physical or visual form?**

## What a first Bradford-on-Avon piece could be

Imagine a large drawing made from thousands of fine marks. Not a map, not a skyline, not a cloud of words.

You run the place name through an inspectable model and capture its internal numerical activity. Tools such as Gemma Scope provide access to layer-specific representations and more interpretable features, so there is a real technical route to investigating that activity. Then you develop a drawing system around relationships in those measurements. For example:

- **Representations that behave similarly** produce marks that align or gather.
- **Differences between controlled contexts** create shifts in direction, spacing or tension.
- **Patterns that persist across those contexts** remain as a continuous structure through the series.

Those are proposed artistic mappings, not inherent shapes hiding inside the model.

Visually, I could imagine something between a field drawing, an engraving and a piece of fabric: passages of tight order, abrupt changes in rhythm, quieter areas, forms that almost resolve into something recognisable but don’t.

## The trap I’d avoid

Taking a few thousand numbers from an LLM, feeding them into a beautiful pattern generator, and calling the result *“how AI sees Bradford-on-Avon.”*

That could look wonderful, but the relationship to the place might be almost arbitrary. You could generate different patterns from different town names using a random seed without involving AI at all.

For *your particular ambition*, I think the stronger test is:

> **Are we preserving something meaningful about the model’s behaviour, or merely using it to supply variation?**

For example, when two inputs have similar measured representations, the resulting works should retain some corresponding visual relationship. When a change in context substantially alters the measured representation, that should alter the composition in a traceable way—not simply shuffle everything.

You don’t need a scientifically definitive portrait of “what the AI knows.” But you should be able to explain what you measured, what you transformed, and what you chose.

That gives the work a much stronger foundation than mysterious numbers dressed up as machine consciousness.

That isn’t a finding about what the model associates with Bradford-on-Avon; it would be your deliberate choice of material. And I wouldn’t force it just because Anna Lucia works with textiles. But it could make the medium part of the meaning rather than simply borrowing her aesthetic.

A plotted drawing or print might turn out to be more yours. The broader lesson I’d take from her is the relationship between system and material—not necessarily the weave.

Suppose you collect thousands of dates, factory locations, production figures and river measurements, then turn those into an intricate pattern. That could be excellent work. For example, Microsoft’s GraphRAG uses language models to extract entities and relationships from documents, then constructs a network that can be examined across the collection. That establishes a practical route to working with relationships between records, rather than simply counting or plotting their contents. It doesn’t guarantee that every extracted relationship is correct or interesting. ([microsoft.com](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/graphrag-unlocking-llm-discovery-on-narrative-private-data/))

For your work, I’d distinguish:

**“There was too much information for me to draw manually”** from **“the way this information is connected wasn’t something I drew manually.”**

The second feels much closer to what you’re after.

## The strongest possibility: let AI connect across the categories you started with

At the moment, you’ve proposed river, Saxons, textiles and rubber. Those are your organising categories.

A weaker process would ask AI to make a pattern for each, then overlay them. It would essentially execute the interpretation you’ve already made.

A more interesting process would ask: **what relationships emerge when those categories stop being the boundaries?**

As an entirely hypothetical example, suppose your archive contains a description of managing river flow, an account of tension in textile machinery, and a technical description of a rubber component absorbing force. A model might propose an affinity around controlling movement or managing forces. That would be a thematic interpretation—not evidence that the three were historically connected.

Crucially, **we shouldn’t feed it those particular themes and then call their reappearance a discovery.** There are methods for building connected groups and summarising their themes before asking a particular question, so your initial headings needn’t dictate the entire result. You would then have the possibility of a work in which “rubber” isn’t one isolated historical layer. Some of its threads reach towards textiles, some towards water, some towards labour, some towards something you hadn’t thought to investigate.

## Your original idea can still be inside it

You don’t actually have to choose between *historical records* and *the model’s learned representation*.

An embedding model can turn passages from your archive into numerical representations, learned through training, whose similarities can be measured. Those measurements give you one model’s organisation of the passages—not just shared keywords, and not a recovered copy of its training documents. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So the archive supplies the local material, while the trained model supplies a particular way of relating it.

**You’re not asking the model to remember Bradford-on-Avon from nothing. You’re observing what happens when its learned representations encounter Bradford-on-Avon’s records.**

For the artwork, I could imagine the following arrangement:

**Time establishes the layers. Documented places anchor the threads. Model-measured affinities pull different moments towards one another.**

The interpretation becomes physically consequential. It determines which strands converge, where the surface thickens, where a distant period interrupts another, where the fabric folds back on itself.

That feels close to your *Interstellar* reference: not simply displaying successive periods, but making relationships between distant moments alter the space of the work.

The precise translation into thread, depth or tension remains yours. Those shapes aren’t secretly sitting inside the model waiting to be uncovered. And a chatbot’s explanation of why it made a connection isn’t a direct reading of its internal mechanism; interpretability research explicitly distinguishes generated explanations from investigating the underlying computation. ([anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/research/tracing-thoughts-language-model))

But the measured relationships could genuinely constrain what you make. You wouldn’t be choosing every connection to suit the composition.

## The important boundary is between evidence and interpretation

I wouldn’t promise that AI will uncover unknown historical truths. That’s something to test, not an artistic justification to assume.

I would keep a distinction between a **documented connection**, such as two records referring to the same site, and a **model-proposed affinity**, such as two accounts describing comparable processes. Source-linked systems can retain references to the passages behind extracted relationships, making those claims available for checking. A reference makes a connection auditable; it doesn’t automatically make it valid.
