[A1] Former workers remembered constant dirt from soot and carbon black, and a separate canteen room for men covered in black powder. Some jobs, such as checking the bounce of every tennis ball, were very dull. Smoking in the workshops could get you sacked, so breaks were taken outside by the river. A Works Committee existed from 1918.

[B1] In 652 Cenwalh, king of Wessex, fought a battle at a place recorded as Bradonforde be afne, the earliest written reference to the town. By 705 a small monastery headed by St Aldhelm stood on the north-west bank of the river.

[C1] The 'Iron Duke' calender, named after Wellington, was designed in America and built in Britain in 1849 for Kingston Mill. It pressed rubber onto woven cloth to make waterproof sheeting. When Avon demolished the mill in 1972, the machine went to Bristol's new Industrial Museum. It was put back on show at the site in 2016.

[D1] George Spencer was testing rubber for railway springs and shock absorbers. He judged Moulton's product the best for the purpose and set up his own firm in the early 1850s. In 1891 the two businesses merged as George Spencer, Moulton and Co.

[E1] Moulton's start-up depended on American partners and skills. He made an 1847 agreement with the Rider brothers and James Thomas of New York. In 1848 an American engineer, Mr Frost, oversaw the machinery, and an American foreman and forewoman, S. P. Abbot and Amelia Fisher, followed him. Woollen workers who had lost their jobs supplied the local labour.

[F1] The aqueduct's central arch sags slightly, possibly because supporting centring was removed before mortar set or because porous limestone was damaged by frost during building. After the Great Western Railway bought the canal in 1852, cracked stonework was patched with blue brick; the Canal & River Trust later repaired it in harder matching stone.

[G1] Bradford on Avon Museum says Moulton was backed by Goodyear and by his friend Septimus Palairet of Woolley. He picked the town for its empty powered mills and its workers already used to factory work. He supplied buffers, springs and hoses to Brunel's Great Western Railway, and rubber-coated cloth for army capes after the Crimean War began in 1853.

[H1] New Mills, now part of the Kingston Mills group, was built as a woollen mill by James Harper and Thomas Taylor, with 1869 carved on its pediment. Cloth-making there stopped in 1898. Soon afterwards Spencer, Moulton and Co took over the empty buildings and converted them for rubber around 1900. Rubber was made there until 1992.

[J1] Avoncliff Aqueduct, designed by John Rennie, carries the Kennet and Avon Canal over the river on three tall segmental arches, the middle one highest. The canal was closed and drained in 1954 and reopened after restoration in the 1980s; the aqueduct was listed Grade II* in 1988.

[K1] A small square domed building on the bridge, probably of the 1600s, was used as the town lock-up in the eighteenth century and known as a blind house. A stone vault inside forms two cells, and its gilded copper fish weathervane, the Bradford Gudgeon, may be sixteenth-century.

[L1] After Roman rule ended, a round baptistery about 15 feet across was cut into the reception-room mosaic of the eastern villa in the fifth century. The excavators called it the earliest known private chapel in post-Roman Britain; the villas were occupied from the 230s into the fifth century.

[M1] Moulton chose the disused Kingston woollen mills for rubber-making in 1848 because empty factories already fitted for water and steam power stood ready, along with workers used to mill life. Early products included buffers, springs and hoses for the Great Western Railway.

[N1] William of Malmesbury saw the church standing in the 1120s and linked it to St Aldhelm, who died in 709. Its architecture instead points to building in the early eleventh century for the Shaftesbury nuns after the 1001 grant; its rich carving may relate to relics of Edward the Martyr.

[P1] Numbers 1 to 18 Middle Rank are a terrace of weavers' houses built in the late 1600s. Most are two storeys with attic rooms in the gables and stone-mullioned casement windows. Part of the row has since been rebuilt. The terrace was listed Grade II in 1963 for its value to the townscape.

[Q1] The Hall family owned land and were clothiers. They ran the whole business of making cloth, from sheep farmers and shearers to weavers, fullers and buyers. Around 1610 John Hall and his wife Elizabeth built The Hall on medieval foundations, facing a river that powered the family's fulling mills and, later, its looms.

[R1] Margaret Dobson's book A Vanished World recalls the Kingston Mill rubber works at the centre of town. Anyone crossing the Town Bridge passed it, and its hooters could be heard all over town. When it closed in 1992, staff were offered jobs at other company sites, but local shops and pubs lost much of their trade.

[S1] In January 1956 the Avon India Rubber Company of Melksham bought Spencer Moulton outright. The Bradford firm kept its name, and a plan that year moved Avon's general rubber goods production to the Bradford works.

[T1] The Moulton works specialised in rubber for engineering, above all railway springs, bearings, and buffer and draw-bar springs. It also made hose, tubing, belting, washers and valves. Sales of springs and rings alone rose from about £3,600 in 1857 to nearly £58,000 by 1890.

[U1] The name Bradford comes from a wide ford over the Avon, which flows westward through the parish. The crossing served routes running north and south, and the ford stayed in use alongside the later stone bridge until the nineteenth century.

[V1] Teachers noticed lines of parched grass on St Laurence School's football pitch, matching buried walls. Excavations led by Mark Corney of Bristol University revealed two fourth-century Roman villas linked by a long corridor paved with a large mosaic of a vase between dolphins, probably made in Cirencester.

[W1] The town has flooded repeatedly, including in 1925, 1932, 1960, 1968, 1979, 2000, 2003, 2013, 2014, 2023 and 2024; November 2024 brought the highest Avon level since local gauging began in 1998. The floodplain narrows from about 600 to 70 metres where the river cuts into town.

[X1] Barton Bridge is a fourteenth-century former packhorse bridge with four pointed arches reaching a small island, and three triangular cutwaters on its upstream face. A narrower footbridge over a side stream was added in the mid-1800s, and a Second World War pillbox stands on the island.

[Y1] Budbury holds an Iron Age hillfort, partly excavated by Geoffrey Wainwright in the late 1960s, that may have been reused after Roman rule. A twelfth-century text names a battle at Wirtgeornsburh, meaning Vortigern's fort, possibly the West Saxon-British fight of 652.

[Z1] Greenland Mills housed the Sirdar Rubber Works. Sirdar went bankrupt in 1914, and in 1915 the Avon India Rubber Company took over the works and about 300 workers. Avon had started in a run-down cloth mill at Limpley Stoke and later moved to Melksham.

[A2] Historic England's garden listing says that when John Hall II died in 1631 the house went to Rachel Baynton. She married into the Pierrepoint family, later Dukes of Kingston, and the house was renamed Kingston House. By the late 1700s the owners seldom lived there and it served as a farmhouse.

[B2] Shaftesbury Abbey held a large estate centred on Bradford from about 1001 through the Middle Ages, managed from Barton. Tree-ring dating places the great barn's two-tier cruck roof between 1334 and 1379, the barn was standing by 1367, and the nearby granary dates to 1370-90.

[C2] Abbey Mill on Church Street was built as a cloth mill by Richard Gane for Harper, Taylor and Co, and stopped making cloth in 1898. It was later used mainly by the rubber firms Spencer Moulton and then Avon. After the factory closed in 1995, the building was converted into retirement apartments.

[D2] The great barn was probably built in the 1330s for Barton Grange, the home farm of Shaftesbury Abbey, a very wealthy Dorset nunnery. The abbey acquired Bradford's rectory and its tithes in 1332, hence the tithe-barn label; a smaller barn about 43 metres long preceded it around 1300.

[E2] In 1803 women made up about two-fifths of the weavers in Bradford and Trowbridge. Across the county that year, weavers might work 13 to 17 hours a day for roughly 7s 6d to 10s a week.

[F2] The council-backed visitor website says the Saxon Church of St Laurence dates from about AD 700 and was found only in 1871. It also says Saxons had reached the area by 652 and that the town grew around the river ford.

[G2] Kingston Mill was built beside the Avon in 1807. In 1848 Stephen Moulton, who had bought the neighbouring Hall, turned the then-idle mill into a rubber factory. A textile mill close to the Hall drew its water through a leat channelled off the river.

[H2] The bridge was only about ten feet wide until its width was doubled in 1768-9. The lock-up on its upstream side began as a medieval bridge chapel, which the antiquary John Aubrey noted on a visit, and was later rebuilt, perhaps to hold prisoners briefly.

[J2] In 1791 about 500 cloth workers gathered at Westbury House, the home of clothier Joseph Phelps, to protest against a new scribbling machine. Shots fired from the house killed three people, one of them a child. The crowd then burned the machine on the town bridge. The coroner ruled the killings justifiable and awarded Phelps £250.

[K2] In 1805 Thomas Divett, a City of London man, bought the estate. He replaced an old corn mill southwest of the house with a cloth mill and used the house itself as a wool store. The mill later failed in a slump in the town's cloth trade, and the house and land were put up for sale in 1848.

[L2] The Moulton firm's workforce started small and grew slowly: 78 people in 1850, only 52 in 1875, 164 by 1900 and 793 by 1950.

[M2] Canon Jones of Holy Trinity identified the forgotten building in 1856 after reading William of Malmesbury. Other buildings had boxed it in, the chancel had been turned into a three-storey cottage, its arch replaced by a chimney built from the same stones, and an upper room held a school.

[N2] By the early 1500s Avoncliff had a mill on each bank of the Avon, grinding corn and fulling cloth. Around 1791 Moggeridge and Joyce installed water-driven cloth machinery there, perhaps the first locally; from the mid-1800s to the late 1930s both mills shredded old textiles into upholstery flock.

[P2] Linden Homes Western redeveloped the former rubber factory site as Kingston Mills. The scheme, designed by Nash Partnership, has 171 converted and new homes plus shops, a café and restaurant space. In 2012 it won Best Mixed Use at the Sunday Times British Homes Awards.

[Q2] There were disputes over pay before the machine riots. The town's first recorded riot, in November 1726, was against more work for less pay. In 1787 weavers rioted over wages. The clothiers reportedly promised not to build bigger loom shops if the weavers accepted lower rates.

[R2] During 2016 renovation and flood-protection work at Holy Trinity, on the Avon's north bank, archaeologists found early burials under the boiler-house floor, one cut by the 12th-century nave. Radiocarbon dated two to roughly the 800s-900s, pointing to an earlier church, perhaps Aldhelm's minster of about 705.

[S2] In the early 1840s fashion moved towards bright, lightweight, intricately woven fabrics, and demand for the town's traditional cloth fell. Then in 1841 the local bank failed. It had invested heavily in two of the largest mills. More than 400 workers were hit, the workhouse could not cope, and many people left to find work.

[T2] Bradford's clothmaking shrank fast after about 1815. There were about 19 manufacturers in 1820, 14 in 1826 and only 5 by 1833. The town kept to broadcloth while export demand for it fell, and by 1838 it had just four mills employing 418 people.

[U2] The weir near Avoncliff aqueduct, downstream of the town, holds back about two metres of water, and a local book suggests it dates from the late 900s. In 2020 engineers judged a breach likely, which could lower levels up to Greenland Mills by one to two metres, prompting community-funded emergency repairs.

[V2] Town Bridge began in the 1200s as a packhorse crossing, possibly replacing an earlier bridge. Two of its original pointed, ribbed arches survive at the south end on the east side, and in 1400 the Pope supported an appeal for alms to pay for repairs.

[W2] Stephen Moulton was Charles Goodyear's agent. He brought the first samples of vulcanised rubber to Britain and shared them with Thomas Hancock, who won the British vulcanisation patent in 1843, a few weeks ahead of Goodyear. Disputes over patents and rights followed, and in 1848 Moulton started his own firm at Kingston Mill.

[X2] The Spencer Moulton archive at the Wiltshire and Swindon History Centre covers the firm from its founding in 1848 to the Avon takeover in 1956. It holds more than 240 volumes, including a nearly complete run of wage books from 1850 to the 1940s, over 3,600 plans, and photographs of products such as railway buffers.
